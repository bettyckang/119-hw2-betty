1. From the dataflow graph, I can tell that many operations in the 
pipeline such as map can run independently on different elements in the input dataset 
(data parallelism). Partitioning the input data would allow for this, as 
different partitions could be processed simultaneously. I would therefore
expect throughput to increase and latency to decrease as the level of data parallelism 
increases. Assuming even workload distribution and sufficient resources, doubling 
the level of parallelism should halve the latency and double the throughput. However, this 
theoretical expectation does not account for other factors that may impact performance 
associated with parallelism, such as additional overhead.  

2. We do see that the general trends of throughput and latency follow our expectations, 
where throughput and latency generally increase for larger datasets. More specifically, throughput 
initially increases with input size before flattening out while latency continues to increase with 
dataset size. However, the observed values do not exactly match theoretical expectations for performance.
For example, for input size 3000000 and parallelism 1, throughput was 58703.21 items/sec and 
latency was 103322.23 ms. When we moved to parallelism 2 with this same dataset, we got a 
throughput of 107393.69 items/sec and latency of 59816.91 ms. Under the theoretical 
assumptions, we would have expected a throughput of 117406.42 items/s and a latency of 51661.115 ms.
We see diminishing returns as parallelism increases further, with throughput actually
decreasing from 228884.67 items/sec to 221471.48 and latency increasing from 26073.71 ms 
to 29016.74 ms when moving from parallelism 8 to 16. For smaller datasets, 
we actually find that increasing parallelism can worsen performance. For example, for a 
dataset of 1000, moving from parallelism 2 to parallelism 16 decreases throughput from 4100.62 items/sec
to 1244.38 items/sec and increases latency from 467.36 ms to 1607.05 ms. These observations suggest
that the benefits of parallelism on performance depend on dataset size.

3. Conjecture: I conjecture that the realized benefits of data parallelism are limited by factors 
such as overhead and computational limitations that are not incorporated into theoretical performance 
assumptions and that a linear improvement in performance with parallelism is not an accurate 
representation of actual performance. Increasing the number of partitions would require more overhead
in tasks such as task scheduling, partition management, and worker communication. Additional overhead might
also be introduced during operations like general_reduce if needing to aggregate values across different
partitions. For smaller datasets, the overhead associated with more partitions may outweight the benefits 
from increasing parallelism, explaining why we see better performance at parallelism 2 than parallelism 16 
on the input size of 1000. The decrease in performance from parallelism 8 to 16 may be associated with computational 
limitations and increased competition for resources.
